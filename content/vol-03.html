Part I: Systems Foundations · ~20 printed pages · Companion to Volumes 1–2

0. Scope and mental model

This volume covers the network as an architectural component: transport behavior (TCP, QUIC), naming (DNS), the HTTP generations, load balancing at L4 and L7, proxies and service mesh, CDNs, and a systematic approach to network failure analysis. The organizing idea: every layer of the network stack is both an abstraction and a failure domain. Each layer hides complexity from the one above (TCP hides loss; DNS hides addresses; the LB hides instances; the mesh hides retries) — and each hidden mechanism has characteristic pathologies that surface as mysterious symptoms one or two layers up. Effective network debugging is largely the skill of mapping a symptom at layer N to a mechanism at layer N−1. A second recurring theme: at large scale, most "network problems" are actually state problems — connection tables, NAT mappings, DNS caches, and conntrack entries are distributed mutable state with TTLs, and they fail the way state fails: stale, exhausted, or inconsistent.

1. TCP: the machinery under everything

1.1 Connections are state machines with real costs

A TCP connection is established by the three-way handshake (SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK — one round trip before any data), carries ordered reliable bytes with flow control (receiver window) and congestion control (sender-side), and is torn down through FIN states, leaving the closing side in TIME_WAIT for ~60s to absorb stray packets. The practical consequences at scale:

  • Connection setup is the tax on chatty architectures. One RTT for TCP plus one to two RTTs for TLS means a fresh HTTPS connection costs 2–3× the network round trip before byte one. Connection pooling and keep-alives are therefore not optimizations but structural requirements; a service that opens a new connection per request multiplies its own latency and everyone's CPU (TLS handshakes are expensive).
  • TIME_WAIT and ephemeral port exhaustion. A client (or proxy/NAT) making many short-lived connections to one destination consumes an ephemeral port per connection, each held ~60s after close. At a few thousand connections/second the ~28k default ephemeral range exhausts, and connection attempts fail with errors that look like the server is down. Fixes: pooling (again), more source IPs, tuning reuse settings — but the architectural fix is fewer, longer-lived connections.
  • Keepalives vs middleboxes. NATs, firewalls, and cloud LBs silently drop idle-connection state (often 350s or less). A pooled connection idle longer than that is dead without either end knowing; the next request hangs until the TCP retransmission timer gives up — minutes. TCP or application-level keepalives below the smallest middlebox timeout are mandatory hygiene for any long-lived connection crossing infrastructure.

1.2 Congestion control: the algorithm decides your throughput profile

The sender maintains a congestion window (cwnd) limiting unacknowledged bytes in flight; throughput ≈ min(cwnd, receiver window) / RTT. Loss-based algorithms (Reno lineage; CUBIC is the Linux default) grow cwnd until packet loss, interpret loss as congestion, and cut the window — which systematically underuses long-fat paths with any random loss, and fills intermediate buffers until they overflow (bufferbloat: high latency caused by full buffers). BBR instead estimates bottleneck bandwidth and min-RTT and paces to that estimate, tolerating random loss and avoiding buffer-filling — typically large wins on lossy WAN/inter-region paths, with fairness caveats when competing against loss-based flows. Two operational facts worth keeping: slow start means every new (or idle-reset) connection ramps from a small window, so short transfers on fresh connections never reach line rate — another argument for warm pooled connections; and after idle periods, cwnd may reset (slow start after idle), which surprises request/response workloads with periodic latency steps.

1.3 Head-of-line blocking, the recurring villain

TCP delivers an ordered byte stream: one lost segment stalls delivery of every byte after it until retransmission completes, even if those bytes belong to logically independent messages multiplexed on the connection. This is transport-level head-of-line (HOL) blocking, and it is the specific defect that motivated QUIC (§2). It also appears in disguise elsewhere: a slow consumer on a multiplexed gRPC channel, a single stalled partition blocking an ordered consumer, one slow shard in a scatter-gather — the general lesson is that multiplexing independent work onto an ordered channel couples the work's fates.

2. QUIC and the HTTP generations

2.1 What QUIC actually changes

QUIC is a transport over UDP, in user space, with TLS 1.3 built into its handshake. The deltas that matter architecturally:

  • Independent streams: loss on one stream never blocks another — transport HOL blocking is gone (HTTP/3's reason to exist).
  • 1-RTT combined handshake, 0-RTT resumption: transport + crypto establish together; resumed sessions can send data in the first flight. (0-RTT data is replayable — servers must restrict it to idempotent requests; a nice example of a transport feature imposing an application-semantics obligation.)
  • Connection IDs, not 4-tuples: a connection survives client IP/port changes (Wi-Fi→cellular migration; NAT rebinding), which TCP cannot do.
  • Costs: higher CPU per byte than kernel TCP (user-space processing, per-packet crypto; improving with offloads), UDP being blocked or de-prioritized on some networks (clients must race/fall back to TCP), and middleboxes losing visibility (encrypted transport headers — a deliberate anti-ossification choice that complicates enterprise inspection).

3. DNS: naming as a distributed cache

3.1 Resolution and its state

Fig 2 — DNS resolution is a hierarchy of caches; propagation delay is TTL arithmetic, not mystery.

3.2 The operational physics of DNS

  • TTL is a failover-speed vs load dial. Low TTLs (30–60s) make DNS-based failover responsive but multiply query load and expose every resolver hiccup; long TTLs pin traffic to dead endpoints. Standard discipline: lower TTLs ahead of planned changes, restore afterward. And measure reality: some ISP resolvers and legacy clients clamp or ignore TTLs, so DNS failover always has a stubborn tail — which is why DNS is a coarse traffic-steering tool, not a precision one, and why real failover designs pair it with anycast or client-side endpoint lists.
  • Negative caching: NXDOMAIN answers are cached too (per the SOA minimum). Querying a name before it exists poisons resolvers with cached absence — a classic self-inflicted launch-day wound.
  • Anycast: the same IP announced from many sites via BGP; routing delivers each client to the "nearest" site. This is how root servers, public resolvers, and CDN edges achieve both proximity and DDoS dilution. Caveat: routing changes can shift a client mid-flow, which is fine for stateless UDP DNS and is one more reason QUIC's connection IDs matter at anycast edges.
  • GeoDNS / weighted / health-checked answers make DNS the global load balancer of record for multi-region entry (route by geography, shift weights for canary or evacuation, drop unhealthy regions). Its precision limits come from resolver granularity: the authoritative server sees the resolver's location (mitigated by EDNS Client Subnet), and one corporate resolver may front an entire company.
  • DNS in service discovery: inside platforms, DNS names backed by service registries (Kubernetes cluster DNS, Consul) inherit all of the above — notably client libraries that resolve once at startup and never re-resolve, which turns any backend IP change into a slow-burn outage. Client resolution behavior (respect TTLs, re-resolve on connection failure) deserves explicit review.
Recurring incident shape: a dependency's IPs change (LB scale event, region evacuation). Services using well-behaved HTTP clients recover in seconds; one fleet with a JVM configured to cache DNS forever (historical default: cache successful lookups for the process lifetime) keeps connecting to the old IPs until every instance is restarted. The postmortem line item is always the same: inventory DNS caching behavior per runtime, and set explicit caching policies — resolution behavior is code, not infrastructure.

4. Load balancing: L4, L7, and the algorithms between

4.1 The two layers do different jobs

L4 (transport)L7 (application)
SeesIPs, ports, connections/flowsRequests: paths, headers, methods, gRPC services
BalancesConnections (per-flow hashing)Individual requests (can split one connection's requests across backends)
Typical formCloud NLB, IPVS, Maglev/Katran-class ECMP + consistent hashing, often with direct server returnEnvoy/NGINX/HAProxy/ALB: routing rules, retries, timeouts, TLS termination, header rewrites
StrengthsEnormous throughput, low latency, protocol-agnostic, preserves end-to-end TLS if desiredContent routing, per-request balancing (crucial for HTTP/2 and gRPC), observability, resilience policies
CostCoarse: a long-lived heavy connection is invisible to itCPU (parsing + TLS), added hop, config complexity that becomes an outage surface

The canonical large-scale stack layers them: anycast/DNS steers to a region → L4 tier (ECMP across stateless-ish forwarders using consistent hashing so any forwarder sends a flow to the same backend) → L7 tier (routing, policy, retries) → services. One detail with outsized consequences: gRPC/HTTP-2 traffic through an L4 balancer alone concentrates load, because many requests share few long-lived connections — per-request (L7) balancing or client-side balancing with a lookaside control plane is required for even distribution.

4.2 Balancing algorithms and their failure content

  • Round robin / least connections: fine for homogeneous requests; least-connections self-corrects for slow backends but can dogpile a newly added (empty) backend — hence slow-start ramping on mature LBs.
  • The power of two choices: sample two random backends, pick the less loaded — nearly all the benefit of global least-loaded with none of the herd behavior of everyone chasing the same "best" backend on stale load data. The default answer for distributed client-side balancing.
  • Consistent hashing / ring hash / Maglev: stable request→backend mapping that survives membership churn — essential when backends hold per-key state (caches, session shards); Maglev-style table hashing gives better balance than classic rings at the cost of slightly more disruption on change.
  • Session affinity ("sticky sessions") is best understood as a liability accepted knowingly: it fights even distribution, complicates drain/deploy, and turns backend loss into user-visible state loss unless state is externalized anyway — at which point stickiness is often unnecessary.

4.3 Health checking: the LB's model of truth

Health checks decide the difference between routing around failure and amplifying it. The essential distinctions: liveness (process up) vs readiness (able to serve now — dependencies warm, caches primed, not draining); active checks (probe endpoint — keep it cheap and dependency-shallow, or the check fails when a soft dependency blips and the LB deserts a healthy fleet) vs passive outlier detection (eject backends whose real-traffic error/latency profile deviates — the practical gray-failure detector, Volume 1 §7). Two safety properties mature systems add: panic thresholds (if "too many" backends look unhealthy, the checker is probably wrong — fail open and route to all rather than concentrate all traffic on the last two "healthy" instances and kill them too) and connection draining on shutdown (stop new work, finish in-flight within a deadline) wired into deploy tooling, so every rollout isn't a micro-outage.

5. Proxies and service mesh

A service mesh moves per-hop network policy — mTLS, retries, timeouts, circuit breaking, load balancing, telemetry — out of application libraries into infrastructure: classically a sidecar proxy (Envoy) per workload programmed by a control plane (Istio-class), increasingly sidecarless variants (eBPF-based data planes, ambient/node-level proxies) that cut the per-pod resource and latency tax. The honest ledger:

  • What it buys: uniform mTLS and identity (SPIFFE-style) without touching app code; consistent resilience policy across polyglot services; per-hop golden-signal telemetry; traffic shifting (canary, mirroring) as configuration.
  • What it costs: a proxy hop's latency (sub-millisecond each, but twice per call, times call depth); fleet-wide CPU/memory for proxies; an additional distributed system whose control plane can now cause a global outage class of its own; and configuration surface (a mistyped retry policy is a self-DDoS one YAML away).
  • The judgment line: mesh value scales with service count × polyglot-ness × compliance pressure (mTLS-everywhere mandates). Small homogeneous estates get most of the benefit from a good shared client library; large heterogeneous ones can't, which is the actual case for mesh. Either way, retries/timeouts configured in the mesh and in applications multiply — one layer must own each policy, explicitly.
Architecture review questions: "For gRPC/HTTP-2: where does per-request balancing happen — and if the answer is 'the NLB', expect hot backends." · "Show the health-check dependency graph: what makes readiness fail, and can a soft dependency's blip desert the fleet? Is there a fail-open panic threshold?" · "Which single layer owns retries and timeouts on each hop, and what is the worst-case multiplication if two layers both retry?" · "What happens during a rolling deploy — drain deadline, in-flight handling, and the measured error rate of a routine rollout?"

6. CDN architecture

6.1 The cache hierarchy

Fig 3 — CDN hierarchy: edge PoPs, an origin-shield tier, and coalesced origin fetches.

6.2 The decisions that determine CDN effectiveness

  • Cache key design: the key is URL + selected headers/cookies/query params. Every extraneous key component (marketing query params, session cookies on static assets, unnormalized parameter order) fragments the cache and silently converts hits to misses. Cache-key normalization is the highest-ROI CDN configuration work.
  • TTL strategy — immutable vs event-driven: the robust pattern is content-hashed asset URLs cached effectively forever (deploys change the URL, not the object), plus short-TTL-or-purged HTML/API responses. stale-while-revalidate and stale-if-error convert origin latency and origin outages into staleness — usually the right trade, and worth setting deliberately rather than inheriting defaults.
  • Invalidation: purge-by-URL is dependable; purge-by-tag/surrogate-key (tag every page containing product X; purge the tag when X changes) is what makes caching dynamic content tractable. Global purge latency (seconds to tens of seconds, provider-dependent) sets the floor on "how stale can a price be" conversations — which are product decisions to surface, not infrastructure trivia.
  • Dynamic acceleration: even uncacheable APIs benefit from riding the CDN: TLS terminates near the client, and the edge→origin leg uses warm connections over managed backbones. Edge compute (request rewriting, A/B bucketing, auth token verification at the edge) moves logic to where the RTTs are cheapest.
  • Failure posture: a CDN is a giant shared dependency with its own incidents; multi-CDN (DNS-steered) is the hedge, priced in operational complexity (config parity across providers is the hidden cost). Serve-stale-on-origin-failure should be an explicit, tested behavior — it is the CDN's graceful-degradation superpower.

7. Network failure analysis: a systematic method

7.1 Where packets actually die

A request path traverses: client stack → NAT/firewall state → DNS answer → anycast/BGP routing → L4 tier (conntrack/flow tables) → L7 proxies (connection pools, buffers) → host virtual networking (veth/bridge/eBPF, security groups) → kernel (accept queues, socket buffers) → application threads. Each hop holds state with limits; the recurring failure families:

FamilySymptom signatureMechanism & first checks
State-table exhaustionNew connections fail/timeout under load; existing ones fineconntrack table full (dropped SYNs, kernel logs), NAT port exhaustion, LB flow-table limits; check counters before blaming the app
Idle-state expiryFirst request after a quiet period hangs for minutes, then worksMiddlebox dropped idle-connection state; keepalive interval vs middlebox timeout audit (§1.1)
MTU / PMTU black holesSmall requests fine; large payloads/TLS handshakes hang — often only on some paths (VPN, tunnel, one AZ pair)ICMP "frag needed" blocked so PMTU discovery fails; overlay/tunnel encapsulation shrinking effective MTU; test with sized pings, check MSS clamping
Accept-queue overflowRetransmitted SYNs, client-side connect latency spikes while server CPU looks fineBacklog full because the app accepts too slowly; ListenOverflows/ListenDrops counters tell the truth
Asymmetric / partial partitionA→B fails while B→A works; only some pairs affected; "works from my machine"ECMP hashing one direction onto a bad path; a single bad link/switch; probe per-path, not per-endpoint (mesh telemetry or pairwise pings)
Retransmission-induced latencyp99 latency at suspiciously quantized values (~200ms, 1s, 3s steps)Those are retransmission timers — small loss rates surface as latency modes, not as errors; check retrans counters per flow direction

7.2 The debugging discipline

  1. Fix the topology first: draw the actual path including every proxy, NAT, mesh sidecar, and overlay — most wrong theories die at this step.
  2. Bisect by layer: does DNS return the right answer (dig from the affected vantage)? Does TCP connect (nc/ss)? Does TLS complete (openssl s_client)? Does the request succeed (curl -v with timing breakdown)? The first failing layer localizes the mechanism.
  3. Compare vantage points: the same probe from inside the pod, the node, another AZ, and outside the platform — differences between vantages are the diagnosis.
  4. Trust counters over narratives: retransmits, conntrack drops, listen overflows, socket-buffer pressure (ss -ti, nstat, LB/mesh metrics) distinguish loss vs delay vs rejection — three different diseases with similar symptoms.
  5. Capture last: a targeted tcpdump at two points (did it leave A? did it reach B?) settles arguments that dashboards cannot; at scale, always-on flow logs and eBPF-based per-hop tracing make this step cheap.
Architecture review questions: "For each hop on the golden path: what stateful limit exists (conntrack, NAT ports, flow tables, accept backlog), what is current headroom, and what alerts at 70%?" · "What is the effective MTU end-to-end across overlays/tunnels, and is MSS clamped where ICMP may be filtered?" · "Can per-path (not per-endpoint) health be observed between AZs/regions — how would an asymmetric partial partition present in the dashboards?" · "Which p99 latency modes correspond to retransmission timers, and would loss show up as latency or as errors in the SLIs?"

8. Architecture review checklist — networking

  1. Connection strategy per hop: pooling, keep-alives below middlebox idle timeouts, reconnect jitter, TLS resumption.
  2. HOL exposure: what independent work shares an ordered channel (HTTP/2 on lossy paths, ordered partitions, scatter-gather) and what decouples it.
  3. DNS behavior as code: per-runtime caching policy, re-resolution on failure, TTL plan for changes, negative-caching awareness.
  4. Per-request balancing for multiplexed protocols; two-choices or consistent hashing chosen deliberately; sticky sessions justified or removed.
  5. Health-check design: readiness vs liveness separation, shallow active checks, passive outlier ejection, fail-open panic threshold, drain wired into deploys.
  6. Retry/timeout ownership: exactly one layer per hop owns each policy; worst-case retry multiplication computed.
  7. CDN: normalized cache keys, immutable-asset pattern, tag-based purge, serve-stale posture tested, origin-shield coalescing on.
  8. State-table inventory: conntrack/NAT/flow/backlog limits known, monitored, alarmed with headroom.
  9. Path observability: per-path probes between zones, retransmit/drop counters in dashboards next to latency.
  10. Failure rehearsal: the last exercised drill for LB failover, CDN origin failure (stale-serve), and DNS-based region evacuation — dated.

  • "The Tail at Scale" — Dean & Barroso — hedged requests and why per-hop variability compounds.

  • Envoy documentation's architecture sections — the clearest practical treatment of L7 balancing, outlier detection, and panic routing.

  • "BBR: Congestion-Based Congestion Control" (ACM Queue) — why loss-based congestion control underperforms and what replaced it.

10. Chapter summary — ten sentences to retain

  1. Every network layer is an abstraction and a failure domain; debugging maps a symptom at layer N to a mechanism at layer N−1.
  2. Most large-scale "network problems" are state problems: connection tables, caches, and NAT mappings that go stale, exhaust, or disagree.
  3. Handshakes cost RTTs, so pooling, keep-alives (below middlebox timeouts), and TLS resumption are structural, not optional.
  4. Ordered channels couple the fates of multiplexed work; QUIC's per-stream independence is the pattern, and it recurs well beyond transport.
  5. DNS is a hierarchy of caches — changes propagate at TTL speed, resolution behavior is code, and DNS steers traffic coarsely, not precisely.
  6. L4 spreads connections; L7 spreads requests — multiplexed protocols (gRPC) need L7 or client-side balancing, or backends run hot.
  7. Health checking needs readiness/liveness separation, passive outlier detection for gray failures, and fail-open panic behavior.
  8. Exactly one layer owns each retry and timeout policy per hop; stacked policies multiply into self-inflicted overload.
  9. CDN effectiveness is cache-key hygiene, immutable assets, tag-based purge, and a deliberate serve-stale posture.
  10. Small loss rates present as quantized latency modes, not errors — retransmit counters belong on the same dashboard as p99.
