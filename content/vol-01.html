A Distinguished Engineer's working reference · Part I: Systems Foundations · ~20 printed pages

0. The DE lens on this material

As a Principal you designed distributed systems. As a DE you will mostly review them, arbitrate disagreements about them, and be the person the VP asks "can this actually work?" The difference in required knowledge is subtle but real: you need less recall of API details and more command of invariants, failure modes, and the boundaries of what is possible. When two Principals disagree about whether a design needs consensus, you must be able to reduce the argument to first principles in front of them.

This volume is therefore organized around reasoning tools, not technologies. Each section ends with a "What a DE probes" box — the questions that expose weak designs in review.

1. First principles that never expire

1.1 The two hard problems

Every distributed-systems difficulty reduces to two facts: (a) messages between nodes can be delayed, reordered, duplicated, or lost, and (b) you cannot distinguish a slow node from a dead one. Everything else — consensus, leases, timeouts, retries, fencing tokens — is machinery for living with these two facts. When a design confuses you, ask which of the two facts it is fighting.

1.2 The end-to-end argument

Reliability guarantees provided by lower layers (TCP delivery, Kafka acks, service-mesh retries) are optimizations, not correctness. Only the endpoints of a business operation can verify that the operation actually happened. Corollary: a "reliable" message bus does not remove the need for application-level idempotency and reconciliation. This single principle kills about a third of the flawed designs you will review — the ones that say "Kafka guarantees delivery, so we're done."

1.3 CAP is a starting point; PACELC is the useful form

CAP says that during a network Partition you must choose between Consistency and Availability. It is widely misused: partitions are rare, so CAP says little about normal operation. PACELC completes it: during a Partition choose A or C; Else (normal operation) choose between Latency and Consistency. Most real design debates are actually EL-vs-EC debates — "do we pay a cross-region round trip on every write?" — not partition debates.

SystemPartition behaviorNormal-operation trade
Spanner / CockroachDBPC — minority side stops serving writesEC — pays commit-wait / quorum latency for consistency
DynamoDB (eventually-consistent reads)PA — keeps servingEL — fast reads, possibly stale
Classic MySQL async replicaPA for reads, PC-ish for writesEL — replica lag is the price
ZooKeeper / etcdPC — minority rejectsEC — linearizable, quorum on writes
What a DE probes: "Show me the partition behavior column for your design. If region A cannot reach region B for 10 minutes, which side serves writes, and what happens to the writes the other side rejected — or worse, accepted?" A design that has no answer has not been designed; it has been assembled.

2. Time, clocks, and ordering

2.1 Physical clocks lie

NTP-synchronized clocks on commodity hardware drift and step; assume error bounds of milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, occasionally worse. Consequences: timestamps from different machines cannot order events that are closer together than the clock error; last-write-wins conflict resolution based on wall clocks silently drops writes; and leases/timeouts computed across machines need safety margins. Google's TrueTime made the error bound explicit (an interval, not a point) and Spanner literally waits out the uncertainty before commit ("commit wait") — that is what buying global external consistency costs.

2.2 Logical clocks: the vocabulary of "happened before"

  • Lamport clocks — a single counter; gives a total order consistent with causality, but cannot tell you whether two events are concurrent.
  • Vector clocks — one counter per node; can detect concurrency (and thus conflicts), at O(n) metadata cost. This is why Dynamo-style stores return "sibling" versions for the application to merge.
  • Hybrid Logical Clocks (HLC) — wall-clock-shaped timestamps that also respect causality; used by CockroachDB, MongoDB, YugabyteDB. The pragmatic modern default: human-readable, bounded size, causally sound.
Fig 1 — Vector clocks: message sends merge clocks; incomparable vectors mean concurrent events (a potential conflict to merge, not an ordering to pick).
What a DE probes: "Where in this system do you compare timestamps produced by different machines, and what happens when they're wrong by 50 ms?" Any last-write-wins resolution, TTL enforcement, or cross-node lease is a place a clock bug becomes a data-loss bug.

3. Consensus

3.1 What consensus is actually for

Consensus lets a group of unreliable machines agree on a single value (or a log of values) despite crashes and partitions. FLP impossibility says you cannot guarantee both safety and liveness in a fully asynchronous system — practical protocols keep safety always and accept that liveness depends on timing assumptions (a leader being elected and staying up). This asymmetry is the design ethic of the whole field: never corrupt state; occasionally refuse to make progress.

3.4 DE-level judgment on consensus

  • Consensus is expensive and precious — use it narrowly. The mature pattern is consensus for metadata and coordination (who is leader, shard maps, configuration, locks) and cheaper replication for bulk data. Kafka uses KRaft for the controller quorum, not for every message.
  • Never let teams build their own. A homegrown "simple leader election" using Redis SETNX or a DB row is where split-brain incidents come from. The review question: "what fences a deposed leader that doesn't know it was deposed?" (See fencing tokens, §4.4.)
  • Quorum size is a latency/availability dial. 3 nodes tolerate 1 failure; 5 tolerate 2 but every commit waits for 3 acks. Cross-region quorums put a speed-of-light floor under write latency — this is a PACELC EL/EC decision made physical.
  • Reads need care too. A "read from the leader" is only linearizable if the leader confirms it is still leader (lease-based reads or read-index), otherwise a deposed leader serves stale data confidently.
Pattern to recognize: a team runs a 3-node etcd/ZooKeeper for coordination, then colocates it with a chatty workload. Disk latency spikes → heartbeats miss → leader elections churn → every dependent service's "coordination" flaps. Consensus clusters need isolated, low-latency disks and their own capacity story. Half of all "ZooKeeper is flaky" complaints are actually "we starved ZooKeeper's fsync."

4. Replication

4.1 The three families

4.2 Quorum overlap, and what it does not buy

Fig 3 — W+R>N guarantees the read sees some replica with the latest acknowledged write — it does not by itself give linearizability or conflict-freedom.

4.3 Replication lag anomalies (the interview-question trio, seen in production weekly)

  • Read-your-writes violation: user updates profile, refresh hits a lagging replica, edit "disappears." Fixes: session stickiness to leader for a window, timestamp/LSN tokens ("read at least up to X"), or read-from-leader for the writing user.
  • Monotonic reads violation: successive reads hit replicas with different lag; time appears to go backwards. Fix: pin a session to one replica or carry a watermark.
  • Causal violation: answer replicates before question. Fix: causal consistency (rare natively) or design so dependent reads carry the causal token.

4.4 Fencing tokens — the pattern that saves failovers

Any lease/lock/leader design must answer: what if the old holder is paused (GC, VM migration, network blip), the lease expires, a new holder proceeds, and then the old one wakes up and keeps writing? The answer is a monotonically increasing token issued with the lease, and downstream systems that reject writes bearing an older token. Without the reject step, the token is decoration. This is also why "we'll just use a distributed lock" is not a complete sentence in an architecture review.

What a DE probes: "Walk me through failover, second by second. Which writes were acknowledged but not replicated? Who decides the new leader — and what stops the old one from writing? What does the client library do during the gap?" The quality of a replication design lives entirely in its failover story.

Fig 4 — Consistent hashing with virtual nodes. Kafka, Redis Cluster, and DynamoDB all use the "many fixed partitions, remap ownership" variant.

5.3 The problems that survive good partitioning

  • Hot keys. One tenant/SKU/celebrity exceeding a partition's capacity. Mitigations: key salting (split the key, aggregate on read), request coalescing, dedicated caching tier for the head of the distribution. Retail traffic is brutally Zipfian — a DE at Walmart scale should assume the top 0.1% of keys dominate and ask every sharded design for its hot-key plan.
  • Cross-shard operations. Secondary indexes become scatter-gather or must be materialized per-partition; transactions across shards need 2PC or saga-style decomposition (Volume 2 covers both). The best mitigation is upstream: choose the partition key so that the dominant transaction touches one shard.
  • Resharding as a live operation. Doubling partitions on a stateful system under load is a project, not a config change. Probe: "what is your resharding runbook, and when did you last exercise it?"

6. Consistency models — a spectrum, not a binary

Fig 5 — The consistency spectrum. The engineering move is matching each data flow to the weakest model its invariant tolerates.

Three DE-level observations that cut through most consistency debates:

  • Consistency is per-invariant, not per-system. "Inventory can't go negative" needs coordination on the decrement path; "product description is current" tolerates seconds of staleness. Systems that pick one model globally either overpay everywhere or corrupt something somewhere.
  • Session guarantees are the sweet spot for user-facing flows. Users don't observe global consistency; they observe their own session. Read-your-writes + monotonic reads make an eventually-consistent backend feel consistent to each user at a fraction of linearizability's cost.
  • CRDTs solve convergence, not invariants. Conflict-free replicated data types guarantee replicas merge to the same value without coordination — great for counters, sets, presence, collaborative editing. They cannot enforce global constraints ("at most one," "non-negative"): those need consensus somewhere, however small.

7. A taxonomy of failure

Junior engineers design for crash-stop. Production delivers stranger things. The DE's job in review is to name the failure classes the design silently assumes away:

Failure classWhat it looks likeDesign counter
Crash-stopNode dies cleanlyReplication, restart, health checks — the easy case
Gray failure / fail-slowNode is "up" but degraded: 1 bad NIC, 99th-percentile disk, one flaky AZ link. Health checks pass; customers sufferDifferential observability (compare the node's self-view vs peers' view of it), outlier ejection in LBs, latency-based health, not just liveness
Partial network partitionA can reach C, B cannot; asymmetric routesConsensus-based membership rather than gossip alone; design for "disagreeing views of who is alive"
Byzantine-ish corruptionBit flips, buggy node serializing garbage, wrong-but-confident responsesChecksums end-to-end, request/response validation, canary comparison
Metastable failureSystem is stable, a trigger (deploy, spike, GC pause) tips it into a bad equilibrium that persists after the trigger ends — usually retry- or cache-drivenSee §9; load shedding, retry budgets, cache priming discipline
Cascading failureOne component's failure overloads the next (thundering herd onto the survivors)Bulkheads, circuit breakers, per-dependency quotas, graceful degradation
Metastable failure, canonical shape: cache cluster restarts → hit rate drops from 98% to 0 → database receives 50× load → DB latency explodes → requests time out → clients retry → load doubles again → even after the cache is warm, the retry storm sustains the overload. The trigger is gone; the failure persists. The fix was never "bigger DB" — it is bounded retries with budgets, request hedging discipline, and shedding load at the front door until hit rate recovers. Every large system has this failure mode latent in it; the review question is "what breaks the feedback loop?"

8. Idempotency and delivery semantics

8.1 The only honest delivery guarantee

Between two processes over a network, you can have at-most-once (send, don't retry — may lose) or at-least-once (retry until acked — may duplicate). "Exactly-once delivery" does not exist; what exists is exactly-once processing effect: at-least-once delivery combined with deduplication or idempotent application. Kafka's "exactly-once" (idempotent producer + transactional read-process-write) is precisely this, scoped to Kafka-to-Kafka pipelines — the moment a side effect leaves that boundary (an email, a payment call), the end-to-end argument applies again and you need your own idempotency.

8.2 The idempotency toolkit

  • Idempotency keys: client generates a unique key per logical operation; server stores key → result and replays the stored result on retry. The subtleties reviews miss: key scope (per operation, not per request), storage TTL vs retry window, and what happens when the first attempt is still in flight when the retry arrives (need a lock or "in-progress" state, or you double-execute).
  • Natural idempotency: design mutations as absolute state ("set quantity to 7") rather than relative deltas ("decrement by 1") where the domain allows.
  • Conditional writes: compare-and-set with a version number — the database enforces "apply only if unchanged," turning duplicates into no-ops.
  • The transactional outbox: the answer to "how do I update my database AND publish an event atomically" — see Fig 6. The dual-write anti-pattern (write DB, then publish to Kafka as two separate acts) is the single most common correctness bug in event-driven architectures; some fraction of one of the two always fails.
Fig 6 — Transactional outbox. State change and event creation share one transaction; the relay makes publishing reliable; consumers make it idempotent.
What a DE probes: "Point to every place a request can be retried — client SDK, gateway, mesh, application, consumer. Now point to the one place that deduplicates. If there are five retry layers and zero dedup layers, this system double-charges someone eventually." Also: "What is the idempotency story for the human retry — the user who clicks Submit twice, the operator who reruns the job?"

9. Backpressure and overload

9.1 The core law

Every system has a capacity. When offered load exceeds it, the only choices are: queue (borrow time — latency grows, and unbounded queues turn overload into memory exhaustion plus doomed work), shed (reject cheaply and early), or degrade (serve a cheaper answer). A system with none of these chosen explicitly has chosen "collapse" implicitly. Goodput — completed-within-deadline work — is the metric; a system at 100% utilization retrying timed-out requests can have near-zero goodput while looking "busy."

9.2 The mechanisms and their composition

  • Bounded queues everywhere. Queue length is your earliest overload signal and your latency budget made physical (Little's Law: L = λW — a queue of 500 at 100 req/s is 5 seconds of latency, whatever your code intends).
  • Admission control / load shedding at the edge: reject before doing expensive work; prioritize by request class (checkout > browse; paid > batch). Rejecting 10% of traffic cheaply beats serving 100% at 10× latency, because timeouts turn "slow success" into "expensive failure."
  • Retry discipline: exponential backoff with jitter (decorrelates the herd), retry budgets (e.g., retries ≤ 10% of request volume, globally), and retry only where idempotent. Retries amplify load exactly when the system can least afford it: three layers each retrying 3× is a 27× multiplier on a struggling dependency.
  • Circuit breakers + bulkheads: stop calling a failing dependency (fail fast, probe periodically); isolate resource pools per dependency so one slow downstream can't consume every thread.
  • Explicit flow-control protocols: TCP windows, HTTP/2 stream windows, gRPC flow control, Reactive Streams demand signals, Kafka's consumer-driven pull (pull-based consumption is intrinsic backpressure — a major, underappreciated reason event-driven architectures survive spikes).
  • Deadline propagation: the caller's remaining budget travels with the request; a service with 50 ms left doesn't launch a 200 ms subquery. Without this, the leaves of a call tree do work whose results the root already abandoned.
Fig 7 — Backpressure composition. Work flows right; pressure must flow left. Every hop without a bound is a place overload hides until it explodes.
What a DE probes: "What happens at 2× design load? Show me the specific line of code or config that says no." And the follow-up that separates real designs from hopeful ones: "When you shed, what do you shed first, and does the business agree with that ordering?" Load-shedding priority is a product decision wearing an engineering costume — surfacing that is exactly the DE's job.

10. The DE review checklist for any distributed design

  1. Invariants: What must never be violated, and which single component enforces each one?
  2. Partition column: During a network partition, which side serves what, and how does the system reconcile?
  3. Failover script: Second-by-second story: acknowledged-but-unreplicated writes, leader fencing, client behavior in the gap.
  4. Clock dependence: Every cross-machine timestamp comparison, lease, and TTL — with the wrong-by-50ms consequence stated.
  5. Retry × dedup map: Every layer that retries; the one layer that deduplicates; the human-retry story.
  6. Dual writes: Any place two systems are updated without an outbox/CDC/transaction is a bug with a delivery date.
  7. Overload plan: The bound, the shed, the degrade — with the code pointer, and the business sign-off on shedding order.
  8. Gray-failure detection: How is a slow-but-alive node detected and ejected? (Liveness checks alone = not detected.)
  9. Hot-key plan: Top 0.1% of keys — measured, and handled how?
  10. Blast radius: What is the largest thing that fails together (cell? region? shared control plane?), and is that acceptable?

12. Chapter summary — ten sentences to retain

  1. You cannot distinguish slow from dead; every mechanism is a coping strategy for that.
  2. Lower-layer reliability never removes end-to-end idempotency and reconciliation.
  3. PACELC, not CAP: most trade-offs are latency-vs-consistency in normal operation.
  4. Wall clocks order nothing across machines; use HLC/logical clocks, and fear LWW.
  5. Consensus keeps safety always, liveness usually — use it narrowly, never homegrown.
  6. Replication quality is failover quality; demand the second-by-second story and a fencing token.
  7. Consistency is chosen per-invariant; session guarantees are the user-facing sweet spot.
  8. Design for gray, partial, and metastable failures — crash-stop is the easy case.
  9. Exactly-once is at-least-once plus dedup; the outbox pattern kills dual writes.
  10. Bound every queue, budget every retry, shed early by business priority, propagate deadlines.
